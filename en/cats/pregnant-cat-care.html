Pregnant Cat Care & Nutrition Guide: Supporting a Healthy Queen

👨‍⚕️
Reviewed by: Imam Mehedi Hasan, VFA
Veterinary Field Assistant & Livestock Medical Practitioner
Last Updated: August 2026

Key Takeaways

  • A cat's pregnancy (gestation) lasts approximately 63 to 65 days.
  • Transition your pregnant cat to a high-quality kitten formula during the last few weeks of pregnancy to meet her increased calorie needs.
  • Provide a quiet, safe, and warm "queening box" for her to give birth in.
  • If she is actively straining for more than 45-60 minutes without producing a kitten, seek emergency veterinary care immediately.

Finding out your cat (queen) is pregnant can be an exciting yet anxious time. Proper prenatal care is essential to ensure the health of both the mother and her future kittens. Understanding her nutritional needs and recognizing when veterinary intervention is necessary can save lives.

1. Proper Nutrition for a Pregnant Cat

During pregnancy, and especially during nursing, a mother cat's energy requirements increase dramatically.

  • Transition to Kitten Food: Around the 4th week of pregnancy, begin gradually transitioning her to a high-quality kitten food. Kitten formulas are richer in calories, protein, and calcium, which are essential for fetal development.
  • Frequent Small Meals: As the kittens grow, they press against her stomach, leaving less room for large meals. Offer smaller, more frequent meals, or allow her to free-feed (leave dry food out all day).
  • Hydration: Always ensure she has access to fresh, clean water.

2. Preparing the Nest (Queening Box)

About two weeks before her due date, your cat will start looking for a safe, quiet place to give birth (nesting behavior).

  • The Box: Use a sturdy cardboard box or a laundry basket. The sides should be high enough to keep the kittens in, but low enough for the mother to step over easily.
  • Location: Place it in a warm, quiet, draft-free room with low foot traffic. Keep her food, water, and litter box nearby, but not directly inside the nest.
  • Bedding: Line the box with clean, soft towels or blankets. Avoid using stringy fabrics that kittens could get tangled in.

3. Signs of Labor & Delivery

When the time comes, cats generally handle birth very well on their own. Signs that labor is beginning include:

  • Restlessness, pacing, and excessive vocalization.
  • Refusal to eat during the final 24 hours.
  • A drop in body temperature (below 100°F).
  • Licking her genital area frequently.

Once contractions start, kittens are usually born every 15 to 60 minutes. The mother will break the amniotic sac, chew off the umbilical cord, and clean each kitten.

4. Warning Signs During Labor

While most births go smoothly, dystocia (difficult birth) is a medical emergency.

Emergency Warning: Contact an emergency veterinarian immediately if you observe any of the following:
  • She has been having strong, active contractions (straining) for over 45-60 minutes without producing a kitten.
  • More than 2 hours pass between kittens, and you know there are more inside.
  • There is excessive, bright red bleeding.
  • A kitten is stuck halfway in the birth canal and cannot be delivered.
