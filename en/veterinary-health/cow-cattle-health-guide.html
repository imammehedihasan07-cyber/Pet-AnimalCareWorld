Common Diseases in Cows & Cattle: Prevention & Treatment Guide

👨‍⚕️
Reviewed by: Imam Mehedi Hasan, VFA
Veterinary Field Assistant & Livestock Medical Practitioner
Last Updated: August 2026

Key Takeaways

  • Proper shed hygiene and timely vaccination prevent most major cattle diseases.
  • Bloat (tympanites) is an acute emergency caused by gas accumulation in the rumen.
  • Mastitis severely impacts milk yield and requires immediate antibiotic intervention.
  • Regular deworming and balanced mineral nutrition are critical for farm profitability.

Livestock farming relies heavily on proactive health management. Field-level medical practice shows that most economic losses in cattle farming stem from preventable or easily manageable infectious and metabolic disorders. This guide covers the most frequent conditions encountered in cattle practice.

1. Bloat (Ruminal Tympany)

Bloat occurs when gas becomes trapped in the rumen and reticulum, forming a foam or gas pocket that the animal cannot expel through burping (eructation).

  • Causes: Sudden grazing on lush green legumes (like clover or alfalfa) or consumption of spoiled, moldy feed.
  • Symptoms: Severe distension of the left flank, difficulty breathing, restlessness, and kicking at the belly.
  • Emergency First Aid: Keep the animal moving. In severe cases, a veterinary professional must use a stomach tube or trochar-cannula to release gas immediately. Anti-foaming agents (like vegetable oil or medical bloat syrups) are administered orally.

2. Mastitis (Udder Infection)

Mastitis is an inflammation of the mammary gland, usually caused by bacterial invasion through the teat canal.

  • Symptoms: Swollen, hot, or painful udder; watery or clotted milk; drop in milk production; fever in the cow.
  • Management: Frequent milking out of infected quarters, intra-mammary antibiotic infusions as prescribed by a veterinarian, and maintaining dry, clean bedding in the shed.

3. Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD)

FMD is a highly contagious viral disease affecting cloven-hoofed animals.

  • Symptoms: High fever, excessive drooling (salivation), painful blisters (vesicles) on the tongue, gums, between hooves, and on teats, leading to severe lameness.
  • Treatment & Care: There is no direct cure for the virus; treatment is supportive (antiseptic washes for wounds, soft feed, antibiotics to prevent secondary infections). Strict quarantine and routine vaccination are mandatory.
Farm Safety Warning: Always isolate sick animals immediately to prevent disease outbreaks across the entire herd. Wear gloves and practice strict farm sanitation.

4. General Farm Bio-security & Prevention

Maintain herd health through these core practices:

  • Strict adherence to regional vaccination calendars (FMD, Black Quarter, Anthrax).
  • Routine deworming every 3-4 months.
  • Providing clean drinking water and balanced mineral mixtures.
